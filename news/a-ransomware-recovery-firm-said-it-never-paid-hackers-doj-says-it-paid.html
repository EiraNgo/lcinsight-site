A ransomware "recovery" firm said it never paid hackers. DOJ says it paid $6,000 in bitcoin and billed $13,260

A federal grand jury in Rhode Island indicted the co-founder of a Florida data recovery company on three counts of wire fraud. Prosecutors say the firm secretly paid ransoms and kept the markup, and an FBI sting caught it.

LC Insight Editorial Team3 min read

Key points

  • The Justice Department said on November 25, 2026, that a grand jury in Rhode Island indicted a 52-year-old Colombian national on three counts of wire fraud.
  • Prosecutors allege his Florida company told ransomware victims it had its own decryption technology and never paid attackers, but secretly paid the ransom and charged more.
  • In a June 2026 FBI sting, the company allegedly paid about $6,000 in bitcoin for a decryption key and billed the fake victim about $13,260.
  • Each count carries up to 20 years in prison. An indictment is only an allegation, and the defendant is presumed innocent.

What the DOJ says happened

A federal grand jury in the District of Rhode Island returned an indictment charging a 52-year-old Colombian national with three counts of wire fraud, the Justice Department said on November 25, 2026. He co-founded a Florida company that sold data recovery services to people and businesses hit by ransomware, malware that locks a victim's files until a ransom is paid.

According to the indictment, the company told customers it never negotiated with or paid ransomware attackers and that it had advanced decryption technology. Prosecutors say it had no such technical solution. Instead, it allegedly negotiated with the attackers in secret, paid the ransom, and charged customers more than it paid, keeping the difference. The DOJ says the money helped buy several properties in Florida.

The FBI sting and the bitcoin

In June 2026, the FBI posed as a company hit by ransomware. The firm told the undercover victim it never paid attackers. Prosecutors say the defendant then negotiated with the supposed attacker, who was also an undercover FBI operative, and paid about $6,000 in bitcoin for the decryption key. The firm charged the undercover company about $13,260 for its "decryption technology services".

That is a markup of about $7,260, or 121% on top of the ransom. The release names no wallet addresses and gives no total for the wider scheme or the number of victims.

Arrest and next steps

The defendant was arrested in Las Vegas on October 22 and ordered held, then moved to Rhode Island. On November 20 the court granted pretrial release under strict conditions. The FBI's Boston field office is investigating, and the Criminal Division's Computer Crime and Intellectual Property Section and the U.S. Attorney's Office for Rhode Island are prosecuting. Each count carries a maximum of 20 years in prison.

Why it matters for ransomware victims

In this case the ransom was paid in bitcoin. The DOJ release points to CISA guidance that advises victims against paying ransoms. A recovery firm that pays quietly while claiming it does not leaves victims funding attackers without knowing it, and paying a premium on top.

LC Insight analysis

This section is our reading of the facts above, not a statement of fact.

  • We think the case will push companies to ask recovery firms for proof of how files were unlocked, not just a promise.
  • The sting shows the FBI is willing to play both victim and attacker to test middlemen. The likely result is more scrutiny of ransomware negotiators.
  • A $6,000 ransom is small, but the alleged 121% markup on a single job shows how a quiet middleman could profit at scale.

What's next

  • The arraignment and any plea in Rhode Island federal court.
  • Whether prosecutors name more victims or a total for the scheme.
  • Any civil claims from former customers of the firm.
