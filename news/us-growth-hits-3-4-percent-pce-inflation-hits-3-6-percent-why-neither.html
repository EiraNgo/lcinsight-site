US growth hits 3.4%, PCE inflation hits 3.6%: why neither helps crypto

At 8:30 a.m. ET on October 29, the government said the US economy grew at a 3.4% annual rate in the third quarter and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge rose 3.6% in a year. For crypto traders hoping for cheaper money, both numbers point the wrong way.

LC Insight Editorial Team6 min read

Key points

  • Real GDP grew at a 3.4% annual rate in the third quarter of 2026, up from 2.2% in the second quarter, the Bureau of Economic Analysis said on October 29.
  • The PCE price index, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, rose 0.5% in September and 3.6% from a year earlier, up from 3.4% in August.
  • Core PCE, which leaves out food and energy, rose 3.1% over the year, while real consumer spending barely grew in September.

Two reports on one morning

The Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) published two major reports at 8:30 a.m. ET on Thursday, October 29, 2026. The first was its advance estimate of third-quarter GDP. The second was the September report on personal income and spending, which includes the PCE price index.

Put together, they describe an economy that is growing fast while prices keep rising. Real GDP, the inflation-adjusted value of everything the US produces, grew at an annual rate of 3.4% in the third quarter. That is up from 2.2% in the second quarter. Measured as a plain quarter-over-quarter change, growth was 0.8%.

On prices, the PCE index rose 0.5% in September alone and 3.6% from a year earlier. PCE stands for personal consumption expenditures. The Federal Reserve uses this index to judge whether it is meeting its inflation goal, so it often matters more for interest rates than the better-known consumer price index.

What drove growth

BEA said the third-quarter gain came from higher investment, consumer spending and government spending. Lower exports pulled the other way. Imports rose, led by capital goods other than cars, and imports are subtracted when GDP is calculated.

Investment rose mainly because companies built up inventories and bought equipment, led by information processing equipment. Consumers spent more on services such as health care, restaurants and hotels, and on goods, mainly prescription drugs. Federal spending outside defense rose, which BEA tied to smaller sales of crude oil from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve than in the second quarter. Those sales are subtracted from government spending, so selling less oil lifts the measured figure without directly changing GDP. Exports fell, led by petroleum and related products.

One measure of underlying demand looked even stronger. Real final sales to private domestic purchasers, which strips out trade, inventories and government, grew at a 4.9% rate, up from 4.6% in the second quarter. Current-dollar GDP, before adjusting for inflation, rose at a 5.6% rate.

Inflation sped up in September

The September report showed price pressure picking up. The PCE price index rose 0.5% in the month, faster than the 0.3% gain in August. Over 12 months it climbed 3.6%, compared with 3.4% in the year to August, according to BEA's August report.

Core PCE, which leaves out volatile food and energy prices, rose 0.3% in September, the same pace as a revised August figure (first reported as 0.2%). Over the year it was up 3.1%, compared with 3.0% in August. A core reading above 3% tells the Fed that inflation is not only an energy story.

That fits the picture from the consumer price index. September CPI rose 3.7% from a year earlier, as LC Insight reported, driven largely by energy.

Why the quarterly inflation figure looks lower

Readers may notice a puzzle. The GDP report put PCE inflation for the third quarter at just 2.1%, with core at 2.5%. Those numbers are far below the 3.6% and 3.1% yearly rates in the monthly report.

The two figures measure different things. The GDP figure is the change in the average price level from the second quarter to the third quarter, expressed as an annual rate. The second quarter had been very hot, with PCE prices rising at a 5.0% rate and core at 3.3%, so the third quarter looked calmer by comparison. The monthly report compares September with September 2025, so it still carries the earlier jump. September's own 0.5% monthly rise suggests the calm did not last into the end of the quarter.

Households are paying more for the same things

The spending numbers show strain under the surface. Personal income rose 0.2% in September, or $54.5 billion, and disposable income after taxes also rose 0.2%. Consumer spending rose 0.5%, or $113.1 billion, slower than August's 0.9%.

After adjusting for inflation, real spending rose by less than 0.1%, or $2.6 billion, down from a 0.5% gain in August. In other words, almost all of the extra money people spent in September went into higher prices rather than more goods and services. The personal saving rate stood at 3.8%, with total saving of $911.1 billion.

What it means for crypto

Crypto markets have been trading on interest rate expectations all fall. The Fed raised its target range by a quarter point to 3.75% to 4% on September 16 and held it there in October, when two officials voted for another hike, as LC Insight reported. Strong growth combined with inflation above 3% gives those officials more arguments, not fewer.

Bitcoin has drifted lower. It traded at about $77,700 on CoinGecko at 8 a.m. ET on November 2, with a market value of about $1.56 trillion, roughly 8% lower than a month earlier. The data releases were one input among several in a busy week, so the move cannot be pinned on BEA's numbers alone.

LC Insight analysis

This section is our reading of the facts above, not a statement of fact.

  • The September PCE report matters more for crypto than the GDP headline. A 0.5% monthly jump and core inflation above 3% make it hard for the Fed to sound relaxed, and higher-for-longer rates have weighed on bitcoin all fall.
  • Real spending growth under 0.1% is the quiet warning. If households keep paying more for the same amount of stuff, growth could cool fast, and markets may price that before the Fed does.
  • Both sides of the rate debate will quote the gap between the 2.1% quarterly and 3.6% yearly PCE readings. Expect choppy trading around each data release until the December meeting.

What's next

  • October PCE and CPI reports, the last inflation readings before the Fed's December meeting
  • BEA's second estimate of third-quarter GDP on November 25, which can revise the 3.4% figure
  • Whether core PCE stays above 3% on a 12-month basis
  • Bitcoin's reaction on release mornings at 8:30 a.m. ET
